What a Typical Lesson Here Looks Like

Pickleball in Volusia County has grown fast, and most of the coaching has grown to match the biggest group of players: people who picked up a paddle in the last year or two. A typical lesson or clinic is an hour of fundamentals. Grip, ready position, serve and return, how to dink, a lot of balls fed from a bucket, some games at the end. It’s friendly and structured, and plenty of good coaches here run it well.

That’s not a knock. I bring lessons back down to that level myself when a backhand or footwork needs rebuilding from the ground up. Fundamentals are fundamentals.

What that format usually can’t do is look at your games. Everyone runs the same drill, whether it’s your problem or the problem of the player next to you. Nobody films you in a real game, counts where your lost points ended, or looks at why your reset pops up in the fourth game but not the first. For a newer player that’s fine, because the shots are the problem. For a 3.5 who has been stuck for six months, the shots are rarely the problem.

What’s Different When You Train With Me

1. We start with your real games, not a curriculum

The main thing I do is a team lesson for league teams and regular groups of 4 to 8. You play real games while I film from the side, then I spend the rest of the hour coaching the weak spots I saw. A 3.5 player almost always has one or two leaks, not ten, and most guess wrong about which ones. The player who thinks it’s their dinks usually has a transition-zone problem. The camera settles it in about two rallies. I wrote the whole framework up in Why You’re Stuck at 3.5: The Five Leaks, and there’s a free 17-question diagnosis if you want a first read before we ever meet.

2. Every pair gets its own breakdown

After the lesson I go home and break down each pair’s film on video, usually 20 to 40 minutes with timestamps, pausing on the points that matter: where you were standing, which shot you chose, and what to hit instead. Your team gets the videos in a private YouTube playlist and your notes in a Google Doc, so you can rewatch them before your next match. Even in a group of 8, you hear about your own game. If it’s just you or one friend, a private or two-player lesson works on one skill, and you get a short slow-motion clip of that shot with my breakdown. With three of you, I fill in as your fourth, we play a real game, and I send one video review of it afterward for all three.

A minute from a women’s 3.5 game I broke down: where to stand while your partner serves. Your team’s breakdowns look like this, on your own games.

3. We look at the body behind the shot

This is the part no standard lesson can offer. I’m a licensed Doctor of Chiropractic (Palmer College of Chiropractic Florida, 2025) as well as a PCI Level 1 certified coach, so when a shot breaks down I can see whether it’s the technique, the feet, the hips, or the fact that your legs are gone by game four. A lot of “bad hands” at the kitchen is actually a trunk that isn’t set. A lot of popped-up resets are a knee that won’t bend under fatigue. When that’s the case, the fix is a movement pattern or a strength piece, and I can give you that too. If something hurts, there’s my chiropractic rehab plan, and a full guide to pickleball knee pain.

4. What I teach is what I’m using right now

I still compete: 4.7 DUPR, playing at a 5.0 level, with 20+ tournament medals at 4.5+ since 2023 and 13+ years of racket sports before that. The patterns I ask you to read are the ones I’m reading on Sunday. That matters for a 3.5 to 4.0 jump specifically, because the difference between those levels is decision-making under pressure, and it’s hard to coach a decision you don’t make yourself.

5. The coaching continues between sessions

In-person lessons are one piece. Between them, you can send footage through Crestline and get a voice-and-video breakdown and a drill plan, from anywhere. Most of my players do a lesson, drill for two or three weeks, send a clip, and come back when the leak has moved. That’s faster and cheaper than a standing weekly lesson where you re-learn the same thing.

The short version: a standard lesson teaches you shots. I film your games, find the one pattern costing you points, fix the body mechanics behind it, and every pair leaves with its own breakdown. If you already have the shots and you’re still stuck, that’s the difference you’re paying for.

Who I’m Not the Right Coach For

  • Brand-new players. If you’ve played fewer than a dozen times, take a beginner class or a group clinic first. You need reps and friends more than you need a diagnosis, and you’ll get more per dollar from a group.
  • Players who want a weekly hitting session. I’m happy to feed balls, and I have a ball machine, but if the goal is exercise and a good time rather than a rating jump, a standing social lesson elsewhere will suit you better.
  • Players who want a quick tip and nothing else. The lesson finds the leak; practicing the fix is what moves your rating. It doesn’t take much, even ten minutes before rec play, but it does take some.

If that’s you, no hard feelings. Take the free diagnosis anyway; it’s useful at any level.

Should a beginner take private lessons or a clinic?

A beginner or a player under about 3.0 gets more from a class or clinic: you need reps, rules, and a friendly group more than you need diagnosis. Private lessons start paying off once you are a consistent 3.0 to 3.5 and losing the same kinds of points, because the thing holding you back is usually one specific pattern a coach has to see in your game.

What is different about a lesson with a chiropractor who coaches pickleball?

A standard lesson looks at the shot. I also look at the chain behind it: how your feet, hips, and trunk are set at contact, why a reset pops up when you are tired, why a knee or shoulder starts hurting in the fourth game. Because I’m a licensed Doctor of Chiropractic as well as a PCI certified coach, the fix can include the movement pattern, not just the paddle, and the drill you leave with is built for your body and your leak.

How many lessons does it take to go from 3.5 to 4.0 in pickleball?

Most 3.5 players do not need a long series of lessons. They need one or two sessions to find the leak, a ten-minute drill they can run twice a week, and a check-in a few weeks later to confirm it stuck. A few sessions spread over six to eight weeks is a realistic starting point. The more you practice the fix between sessions, even ten minutes before rec play, the faster it sticks.
