## Pickleball Knee Pain: The 3-Stage Fix

Foam rolling and rest keep the pain quiet for a week. Here’s why it comes back, and the system I use to actually fix it.

Knee pain is one of the most common complaints I see in recreational players over 45, and on most courts I’m on, somebody is managing a nagging knee. The pain shows up at the knee, but in my experience the cause is usually not the knee itself. It’s upstream: weak hips, movement mechanics that overload the joint, and volume spikes. Fix the upstream problem and the knee tends to calm down and stay that way.

Most players try the same three things: foam roll the IT band, stretch and ice, take a week off. All three can make the knee feel better for a little while. None of them fix why it started hurting in the first place, so it comes right back the next time you play a full session. This guide is the 3-stage system I use with players to actually get it to stop, built for people who refuse to just stop playing.

I’m a Doctor of Chiropractic and I play at a 5.0 level myself, so I see this from both sides of the net. As a chiropractor, the knee is one of the most common complaints that walks through the door. As a player, I’ve watched the same pattern happen to partners and opponents for years: they manage the pain instead of fixing it, and it costs them a season instead of a few weeks. This is the framework I built to stop that cycle.

### Why the Pain Keeps Coming Back

Most players are treating the symptom, not the cause. Foam rolling, icing, and resting can all help temporarily. But if the actual cause never gets addressed, the knee flares up again the next time you go back to playing. The knee is where it hurts. The cause is almost always somewhere else in the chain.

The real problems are usually upstream: weak hips, poor movement mechanics, and volume spikes. Not the knee itself. Every low ball you dig out is a squat. Every lateral shuffle is a lunge. If your hips can’t do their job, the knee absorbs load it was never built to handle alone, and it lets you know.

This isn’t just a clinic observation. A 2015 systematic review in the British Journal of Sports Medicine found strong evidence that adding hip and glute rehab to knee-focused rehab reduced pain and improved function in people with patellofemoral (front-of-knee) pain, and that hip work alone beat quad work alone in the short and medium term.1 On the volume side, sports-science work on training load shows that injury risk climbs when the amount you do in a week jumps well above what your body has been used to, not simply when you play a lot.2 That’s the volume spike. And the number of senior pickleball injuries treated in U.S. emergency departments rose sharply from 2010 to 2019, approaching the count for senior tennis injuries by the end of that decade, with strains and sprains among the most common diagnoses, so this is not a small-scale problem.3

#### Why It Comes Back: Four Common Mistakes

- Treating only the symptom. Icing and resting without addressing the movement cause. It feels better, then it returns the moment you're back on court at full volume, because nothing about how you move actually changed.

- Returning too fast. Pain fades, so full play resumes immediately. The knee re-loads before it’s actually ready to handle it, and the same irritation shows back up within a week or two of hard play.

- Skipping hip strength. Weak glutes and hip stabilizers are the most common mechanical driver of recurring knee pain I see, and the stage most players skip because it doesn’t feel urgent once the pain is gone. It's the least exciting stage and the most important one.

- Stopping when it feels good. Strength requires maintenance. Pain relief is not the finish line, it’s the halfway point. The players who stay pain-free are the ones still doing the maintenance work months later.

Self-check: if your knee pain has come back more than once after resting it, you’re dealing with an upstream cause, not a knee problem. That’s exactly what the 3-stage system below is built to fix.

### Stage 1: Reduce Irritation

This doesn’t mean leave the court. It means reducing the load on the knee while keeping the body moving and waking up the muscles that should be doing more of the work. You don’t have to leave the court, just change how you use it while Stage 1 works.

Focus on four things: reduce match play volume without eliminating it, avoid explosive cuts temporarily, activate the hips and glutes starting day one, and drill instead of compete. None of that means sitting on the couch. It means being intentional about what you ask the knee to do while the tissue calms down and the hips start picking up more of the load.

Don’t start this program on your own if: the knee swelled up suddenly, it locks or gives way, the pain started with a fall or a collision, it wakes you up at night, or you can’t put weight on it. Those need to be evaluated in person first. This program is for the nagging, load-related knee that’s been building for weeks.

#### Stage 1 Exercises — start here, 3–4x per week

- Modified Side Plank — activates the lateral hip, reduces the load the knee takes on every lateral cut and shuffle.

- Single Leg Glute Bridges — engages the glutes and teaches the hip to carry load so the knee doesn’t do all the work.

- Modified Copenhagen Plank — builds inner-thigh and hip control, stabilizes knee alignment during the split-step and shuffle.

- Fire Hydrants — targets the glute medius, the primary hip stabilizer that protects the knee during play.

You’re ready for Stage 2 when: walking and stairs are pain-free, all four Stage 1 exercises cause zero knee pain, and drilling feels comfortable.

### Stage 2: Build Strength

This is the stage most players skip. Pain fades, they return to full play, and it comes back within weeks because the knee never actually got the support it needed. The goal here is building the hip and glute strength that takes pressure off the knee during every lateral move, split-step, and push-off.

This is the difference between managing knee pain and actually fixing it. Stage 1 calms the tissue down. Stage 2 is where the knee gets stronger than it was before the pain started, which is the only thing that keeps it from coming back once you're playing full volume again.

#### Stage 2 Exercises — build the foundation, 3–4x per week

- Full Side / Copenhagen Plank — strengthens the hip abductors and adductors, controls leg alignment under every court stance and lunge.

- Single Leg Hip Thruster — builds the glute power that absorbs force before it reaches the knee on every push-off.

- Single Leg Step-Downs — trains the hip to stabilize the knee during the loading phase that happens before every shot.

- Kickstand RDLs — builds posterior chain and single-leg balance that transfers directly to court movement and deceleration.

You’re ready for Stage 3 when: Stage 2 exercises are pain-free and feel genuinely stronger, not just tolerable, and you can drill at real intensity without the knee reacting the next day.

### Stage 3: Return to Full Play

Pain is gone. Strength is built. Now we reintroduce the explosive demands of pickleball, gradually. Even once pain is gone, the knee still needs time to rebuild tolerance to the impact of competitive play, so this stage is a progression, not a light switch.

This is where I see the most players get impatient. Pain-free feels like the finish line, so they go from drilling straight back to three-hour tournament days. The knee has to rebuild tolerance to real match intensity the same way it rebuilt strength in Stage 2: gradually, with a plan, not all at once.

#### Stage 3 Exercises — build power, 2–3x per week

- Single Leg RDLs — single-leg stability and hip control that transfers directly to explosive court cuts and changes of direction.

- Heavy Farmer’s Carries — core and hip stability under load, trains the body to resist the lateral forces of competitive play.

- Bulgarian Split Squats — builds quad and glute strength in a lunge position that mirrors the split-step and court lunge.

- Single Leg Bounds — controlled plyometric loading that prepares the knee for the impact of full competitive play.

Self-check: if any part of this progression brings the knee pain back, that’s not a setback, it’s information. Drop back to the last pain-free week and rebuild from there instead of pushing through it.

### Maintenance: Keep These Forever

Strength requires maintenance. Pain relief is not the finish line. Keep these in rotation 1–2x per week for good, even once you feel completely normal: single-leg hip thrusters, side plank variations, Bulgarian split squats, single-leg RDLs.

### Questions

Is pickleball knee pain usually an IT band problem?

Usually not, even though the IT band is the first thing most players foam roll. The IT band can feel tight and sore because of what’s happening upstream: weak hips, movement mechanics that put extra load through the outside of the knee, or a recent jump in how much you’re playing. Foam rolling the IT band can feel good for a few hours, but if the hips and mechanics behind it never get addressed, the tightness comes right back.

Can I keep playing pickleball with knee pain?

In Stage 1, yes, in most cases. You don’t have to leave the court. You reduce match play volume, avoid explosive cuts temporarily, and drill instead of compete while you calm the knee down and activate the hips. The exception is pain that’s sharp, swelling, or getting worse each session. That’s a sign to get it looked at before you keep loading it.

How long does it take to fix pickleball knee pain?

It depends on the person. The Quick Start Plan is built as a five-week minimum: two weeks reducing irritation, two weeks building strength, then a gradual return to full play from week five on. How long it actually takes depends on how long the knee has been irritated, your starting strength, and how you respond to Stage 1, and some players need considerably longer. Consistency and gradual progression matter more than speed. Players who skip Stage 2 to rush back to full play are usually the ones back in my inbox a few weeks later. If the pain isn’t improving after a real attempt at Stages 1 and 2, get it evaluated in person.

Do I need an MRI for pickleball knee pain, or when should I see someone?

Most pickleball knee pain doesn’t need imaging to start working on it. This guide is educational content, not medical advice, so if pain is sharp, if the knee swells, locks, gives way, or if it isn’t responding after a real attempt at Stage 1 and Stage 2, that’s the point to see a licensed provider and get it properly evaluated instead of guessing further on your own.

Is my pickleball knee pain caused by my shoes?

Footwear can be a small piece of the puzzle, but it’s rarely the actual cause. The pattern I see over and over is weak hips, movement mechanics that load the knee wrong, and volume spikes, not the shoe on your foot. Before you spend money on new shoes, work the 3-stage system here. If the mechanics and strength are solid and something still feels off, footwear is worth a look.
